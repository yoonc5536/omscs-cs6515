SECTION 01

How your answer is graded

The grader is looking for three different things. Keep them visibly separate so none is left implicit.

(a) Algorithm

Describe the method in prose—not code, pseudocode, or a line-by-line translation of code. State the base case, every step, and the final return value. A reader should be able to implement it.

(b) Justification

Argue why the method is correct. Correctness must be explicit here, not hidden inside the algorithm description.

(c) Runtime

Give worst-case Big-O, tie every recurrence term to a concrete step, and simplify to a final bound.

Design constraint

The solution must genuinely use divide and conquer and be asymptotically faster than brute force.

Allowed black boxes

You may use these algorithms as taught: Binary Search O(log n), Merge Sort O(n log n), FastSelect O(n), and FastMultiply O(n^{log₂3}). If you modify one, state the modification, justify the modified behavior, and analyze its runtime impact.

REAL HW2 DEDUCTIONS TO AVOID
  • Vague combine step: “extend outward” does not say where the scan starts, what it compares, or when it stops.
  • Incomplete correctness: listing cases is not enough; show why taking their maximum gives the global optimum.
  • Unexplained recurrence: identify which algorithm step creates each term.
  • Missing labels: tag (a), (b), and (c).
SECTION 02

The answer template

Use this skeleton to turn an idea into a gradable answer without mixing the three jobs.

(a) Algorithm

“If the input size is [base case], return [value]. Otherwise: (1) Divide: … (2) Conquer: recursively … (3) Combine: [precise scan or merge—start position, comparison, stopping condition]. Return [what].”
  • Can someone implement it from the prose?
  • Did you name the base case and final return?
  • Is the combine step operationally precise?

(b) Justification of Correctness

“Every feasible solution falls into exactly one of these cases: …. By induction, the recursive calls solve cases …. The combine step computes …. Therefore the maximum or combination over these cases equals the global optimum.”
  • Are the cases exhaustive?
  • Why are the recursive answers correct?
  • Why does combine find the best crossing or mixed case?

(c) Runtime Analysis

“Dividing costs O(…). The [a] recursive calls on size [n/b] contribute aT(n/b). The combine step costs O(…) because …. Hence T(n)=…=O(…) by the Master Theorem, which beats brute force O(…).”
  • Is the bound worst-case?
  • Is every recurrence term explained?
  • Is the final Big-O fully simplified and asymptotically better?
SECTION 03 · DC3

Recurrences & the Master Theorem

30-SECOND RECAP

For T(n)=aT(n/b)+O(n), compare a with b. Fewer total children than shrink factor: linear. Equal: n log n. More: leaves dominate, giving n^{log_b a}.

EXAM FOCUS

Master rule for linear combine work:
a < b ⟹ O(n)  ·  a = b ⟹ O(n log n)  ·  a > b ⟹ O(n^{log_b a})

Why the rule works

Expanding the recurrence produces cn times a geometric series whose ratio is a/b:

cn · [1 + (a/b) + (a/b)² + ···] + a^{log_b n}

Worked example: expand T(n) = 3T(n/2) + cn

The root contributes cn. One level down there are three subproblems, each costing c(n/2), so that level costs (3/2)cn. At level 2, nine nodes each cost c(n/4), for (3/2)²cn.

EXAM FOCUS · EXPONENT CONVERSION

3^{log₂n} = n^{log₂3}. Write 3=2^{log₂3}:

(2^{log₂3})^{log₂n} = 2^{log₂3 · log₂n} = (2^{log₂n})^{log₂3} = n^{log₂3} ≈ n^1.59

SECTION 04 · DC1

Fast integer multiplication

30-SECOND RECAP

Split each n-bit number in half. Compute only three half-size products, recover the two cross terms algebraically, and combine with shifts. Recurrence: 3T(n/2)+O(n), so runtime is O(n^{log₂3})≈O(n^1.59).

Gauss’s three-product idea

Complex multiplication seems to need four real products:

(a+bi)(c+di) = (ac−bd) + (ad+bc)i

But ad+bc=(a+b)(c+d)−ac−bd. Once ac and bd are known, one additional multiplication recovers both cross terms together. FastMultiply applies the same trick to high and low bit-halves.

(a) Algorithm

Assume n is a power of two. Write

x = 2^{n/2}·x_l + x_r    and    y = 2^{n/2}·y_l + y_r.

If n=1, return x·y. Otherwise recursively compute A=x_l·y_l, B=x_r·y_r, and C=(x_l+x_r)(y_l+y_r). Return:

2^n·A + 2^{n/2}·(C−A−B) + B

Multiplication by a power of two is a bit shift, taking O(n) bit work.

(b) Justification of Correctness

Expanding the split representation gives:

xy = 2^n·x_l y_l + 2^{n/2}(x_l y_r + x_r y_l) + x_r y_r.

Also, C=(x_l+x_r)(y_l+y_r)=A+B+(x_l y_r+x_r y_l). Therefore C−A−B is exactly the missing cross-term sum. Substituting A, B, and C into the return expression gives exactly xy.

(c) Runtime Analysis

The three recursive multiplications are on n/2-bit numbers, giving 3T(n/2). Splitting, additions, subtractions, and shifts take O(n) bit operations.

T(n)=3T(n/2)+O(n)=O(n^{log₂3})≈O(n^1.59)

This is asymptotically faster than grade-school O(n²) multiplication.

EXAM FOCUS

The entire improvement is the reduction from four recursive half-size products to three. Four gives O(n²); three gives O(n^{log₂3}).

Worked example: 182 × 154

Using four-bit halves: x_l=11, x_r=6, y_l=9, y_r=10. Then A=99, B=60, and C=17×19=323.

99·256 + (323−99−60)·16 + 60 = 25,344 + 2,624 + 60 = 28,028 ✓
SECTION 05 · DC2

FastSelect & linear-time median

30-SECOND RECAP

Partition around a pivot and recurse into only the bucket containing rank k. Median-of-medians chooses a pivot that discards a constant fraction in the worst case. With groups of five: T(n)=T(n/5)+T(3n/4)+O(n)=O(n).

(a) Algorithm

Choose a pivot p. In one linear scan, partition A into L={x:x<p}, E={x:x=p}, and H={x:x>p}.

  • If k≤|L|, return FastSelect(L,k).
  • If k≤|L|+|E|, return p.
  • Otherwise return FastSelect(H,k−|L|−|E|).

Pivot selection: divide A into ⌈n/5⌉ groups of five, sort each constant-size group, collect their medians into S, and recursively set p=FastSelect(S,⌈|S|/2⌉).

Worked example: find the 4th smallest value

Let A=[7,2,9,4,5,8,1,6,3,10] and k=4. Sort the two groups of five: [2,4,5,7,9] has median 5, and [1,3,6,8,10] has median 6. The lower median of [5,6] gives pivot p=5.

Partition the original array: L=[2,4,1,3], E=[5], and H=[7,9,8,6,10]. Since k=4≤|L|=4, recurse on (L,4). The 4th smallest value of L is 4.

(b) Justification of Correctness

The k-th smallest element lies in exactly one of L, E, or H. In H, discarding L and E removes exactly |L|+|E| elements no greater than p, so subtracting that amount preserves the target rank.

For pivot quality, p is the median of the group medians. At least n/10 group medians are no greater than p; each has two smaller group-mates, so at least 3n/10 elements are ≤ p. Symmetrically, at least 3n/10 are ≥ p. Thus each side contains at most 7n/10≤3n/4 elements. Each recursive selection therefore discards a constant fraction and terminates with the correct rank.

(c) Runtime Analysis

Grouping and constant-size sorting cost O(n); selecting the median of S costs T(n/5); partitioning costs O(n); and the rank search recurses on at most 3n/4 elements.

T(n)=T(3n/4)+T(n/5)+O(n)=O(n), because 3/4+1/5=0.95<1.
EXAM FOCUS

Keep the two recursive terms separate: one finds the pivot among n/5 medians; the other selects the target rank in a partition of size at most 3n/4.

Why groups of five?

Groups of 3 fail

Each median certifies two elements. At least 2·(n/6)=n/3 lie on each side, so the recurrence is T(2n/3)+T(n/3)+O(n). The fractions sum to 1, giving O(n log n), not linear.

Groups of 5 or 7 work

For 5, the fraction sum is 3/4+1/5=.95<1. For 7, at least 2n/7 lie on each side, so T(5n/7)+T(n/7)+O(n); 6/7<1.

EXAM FOCUS · GROUP SIZE

Groups of 5 and 7 preserve worst-case linear time. Groups of 3 do not.

Random vs. deterministic: a random pivot is good (between ranks n/4 and 3n/4) with probability 1/2, yielding expected O(n) but worst-case O(n²). Median-of-medians guarantees worst-case O(n).

Course quiz drill: 25 elements

Groups: [d,c,b,e,a] [i,f,h,j,g] [l,m,k,n,o] [p,s,q,r,t] [w,x,v,u,y]. Their medians are b, h, k, q, v; sorted, k<h<q<b<v, so the median of medians is q.

  • Guaranteed strictly less than q: k, h, l, m, i, f, p, s—8 elements.
  • If q is 9th smallest and the target is rank 13, recurse above q with rank 13−9=4.
  • For n=25, good pivots have ranks 7 through 18 inclusive: 18−7+1=12 pivots.

SECTION 07 · FULL MODEL ANSWER

Longest Hill

DEFINITION

A hill is a contiguous segment that strictly increases and then strictly decreases. A purely increasing or purely decreasing segment counts. A single element is a hill of length 1. Equal adjacent values can never be crossed by a hill.

This answer demonstrates the exact level of operational detail, case coverage, and recurrence explanation expected on the exam.

(a) Algorithm

If n=1, return 1. Let mid=⌊n/2⌋. Recursively compute L=LongestHill(A[1..mid]) and R=LongestHill(A[mid+1..n]).

To compute the longest crossing candidate, compare A[mid] and A[mid+1]:

  • If equal: set the candidate to 0, because strictness forbids a hill from crossing the boundary.
  • If A[mid]<A[mid+1]: scan left from mid while A[i−1]<A[i], stopping at s. Scan right from mid+1 while A[i]<A[i+1], stopping at q, the peak. Then scan right from q while A[i]>A[i+1], stopping at e. Set candidate to e−s+1.
  • If A[mid]>A[mid+1]: scan right from mid while A[i]>A[i+1], stopping at e. Scan left from mid while A[i−1]>A[i], stopping at s. Then scan left from s while A[i−1]<A[i], stopping at t. Set candidate to e−t+1.

Return max(L,R,candidate).

Worked example: A = [3, 1, 4, 5, 2, 6]

Let mid=3. Because A[3]=4<A[4]=5, use the rising-boundary case. Scanning left from index 3 stops at s=2, because A[1]=3 is not less than A[2]=1. Scanning right from index 4 stops immediately at the peak q=4. The decreasing scan then stops at e=5, because A[5]=2 is not greater than A[6]=6.

The crossing candidate is A[2..5]=[1,4,5,2], length 4. The recursive answers are L=2 on [3,1,4] and R=2 on [5,2,6]. Therefore max(2,2,4)=4.

(b) Justification of Correctness

Every hill lies wholly in the left half, wholly in the right half, or contains the boundary pair; these cases are exhaustive. By induction, L and R are the longest hills of the first two kinds.

For the crossing case with A[mid]<A[mid+1], any crossing hill has a peak at or to the right of mid+1. Its increasing part is contained in the maximal increasing run [s..q] through the boundary. Extending to the whole run and then taking the maximal decreasing run from q can only lengthen it, so the candidate is the longest crossing hill. The A[mid]>A[mid+1] case is symmetric. Therefore, the maximum of L, R, and candidate is the longest hill in the full array.

(c) Runtime Analysis

Splitting costs O(1). The two half-size recursive calls contribute 2T(n/2). During the crossing computation, each scan pointer moves monotonically across at most the current subarray, so all crossing scans together cost O(n). The final maximum costs O(1).

T(n)=2T(n/2)+O(n)=O(n log n)

Here a=b=2, so the Master Theorem gives O(n log n), faster than the O(n²) brute force of checking all subarrays.

SECTION 08 · SHORT MODEL ANSWER

Merging k sorted arrays

Problem: merge k sorted arrays, each containing n elements.

(a) Algorithm

Repeatedly pair the current arrays and MERGE each pair. If one array is unpaired, carry it to the next round. Continue until one array remains; return it.

Worked example: four arrays of two elements

Start with [1,5], [2,6], [3,7], and [4,8]. At level 1, merge adjacent pairs into [1,2,5,6] and [3,4,7,8]. At level 2, merge those results into [1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8].

(b) Justification

MERGE correctly returns the sorted union of two sorted arrays. Every round preserves the full multiset while reducing the number of arrays. Therefore the one remaining array is the sorted union of all k inputs.

(c) Runtime

Every level merges kn elements total in O(kn) time. Pairing halves the number of arrays, so there are log k levels. Total runtime is O(kn log k).

SECTION 09

Quiz review: questions & answers

Read each prompt first, commit to an answer, then compare your reasoning with the highlighted result.

Q1

An algorithm has runtime T(n) = aT(n/b) + O(n). After applying the Master Theorem, you conclude that T(n) = O(n log n). Which of the following must be true about a and b?

Answer: a = b.Why: O(n log n) is the equal-work case of the Master Theorem for linear combine work.
Q2

An algorithm takes as input an array A of length n. After performing a linear-time operation, it recurses to exactly one of A[1...n/2] or A[n/2+1...n]. The tightest bound for its runtime is ____.

Answer: O(n).Why: T(n) = T(n/2) + O(n); the geometrically shrinking linear work sums to O(n).
Q3

An algorithm takes as input a matrix A[1...m][1...n] of dimensions m × n. After performing an O(m) operation, it recurses to exactly one of A[1...m][1...n/2] or A[1...m][n/2+1...n]. The tightest bound for its runtime is ____.

Answer: O(m log n).Why: T(n) = T(n/2) + O(m), with O(m) work at each of log n levels.

Q4

Which of the following numbers are guaranteed to be less than q? (Check all that apply.)

Answer: k, h, l, m, i, f, p, s.Why: these are the two medians below q and their two smaller group-mates, plus q’s two smaller group-mates—8 numbers total.
Q5

You are furthermore told that q is the ninth smallest number. Then FastSelect recurses to ____.

Answer: the set of numbers bigger than q.Why: the median of 25 has rank 13, and 13 > 9.
Q6

In the recursive call referred to in Question 5, what is the rank term passed to FastSelect?

Answer: 4.Why: the adjusted rank is 13 − 9 = 4 after discarding q and the eight elements smaller than it.
Q7

How many of the 25 numbers are good pivots?

Answer: 12.Why: good pivots occupy ranks 7 through 18 inclusive, between n/4 and 3n/4.
Q8

You and your friend Raquel each designed a divide-and-conquer algorithm with two subproblems of half the input size. Your merge step takes 2n+1 time; Raquel’s takes n² time. Check ALL true statements.

Answer: “Your design is faster than Raquel’s design.”Why: yours is 2T(n/2)+O(n)=O(n log n); Raquel’s is 2T(n/2)+O(n²)=O(n²).
Q9

You have k sorted arrays of n elements each. Repeatedly pair them up and MERGE each pair until one array remains. The running time is ____.

Answer: O(kn log k).Why: each level processes kn elements, and pairing produces log k levels.
Q10

Fast multiplication of two n-bit integers uses three recursive multiplications of n/2-bit integers plus linear combine work. Its runtime is ____.

Answer: O(nlog₂3) ≈ O(n1.59).Why: T(n)=3T(n/2)+O(n), so the recursive term dominates.
